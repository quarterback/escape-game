# Boring systems that make good games
## Starting points for the UT330 game sprint

These are systems, not game ideas. A system becomes a game when you pick one
person's perspective inside it, name what they can see and change, and decide
what success means. The same system can produce very different games depending
on whose work you follow.

**What makes a system game-worthy (the parameters):**
- Someone has to complete a task with a clear beginning and end.
- Other people or organizations control steps the player cannot skip.
- Information is unevenly distributed — someone knows something someone else doesn't.
- There is a cost to being wrong, or a cost to waiting.
- The system has a record, a queue, a status, or a schedule the player can read.
- It can be observed firsthand in a day.

---

## Public services and government

**Permit or license application**
A resident needs a permit. The clerk needs documents in a specific order, some
of which come from other offices. What does the applicant see vs. what does the
clerk see? A denied application restarts the queue.

**Building inspection scheduling**
A contractor requests an inspection. The inspector has a route, a calendar, and
a checklist. The contractor doesn't know the route. Missed windows cost money.
Data hook: inspection pass/fail rates by inspector, by district, by season.

**Voting / poll worker shift**
A poll worker sets up, processes voters, and closes. Voters arrive in bursts.
Equipment fails. The worker has to decide what to do when the line is long and
the machine is down.

**Court date / summons**
Someone receives a summons. They need to appear, possibly with documents,
possibly with a lawyer. The system has a docket, a waiting room, and a clerk
who controls the order. Data hook: no-show rates, continuance patterns.

**Library hold pickup**
A hold expires in 7 days. The patron doesn't know the shelf location. The
library has a pull list, a holds shelf, and a check-in queue. Simple system,
real stakes for the patron.

## Housing and neighborhoods

**Rental application / waitlist**
An applicant submits paperwork. The landlord or housing authority checks
income, references, and availability. The applicant waits without knowing their
position. Data hook: wait times, denial reasons, vacancy rates.

**Maintenance request in a large building**
A tenant files a request. It goes to a dispatcher, then a vendor, then back
to the tenant for access. Each handoff has a delay. The tenant can't see the
schedule. Data hook: response times by request type, by building.

**Eviction or code enforcement process**
A notice is issued, a hearing scheduled, a decision made. The person affected
has to navigate deadlines, forms, and appearances. The system has a timeline
the person must follow.

**Neighborhood association meeting**
Someone wants to propose a change (a crosswalk, a tree, a sign). The
association has an agenda, a quorum, a voting rule, and a history of past
decisions. The proposal may be tabled, referred, or rejected.

## Transit and mobility

**Transit transfer**
A rider has 90 seconds to move from one platform to another. The system has
schedules, delays, and a fare boundary. The rider sees a countdown; the
operator sees a network.

**Parking enforcement and dispute**
A ticket is issued. The person can pay or contest. Contesting requires a
hearing, evidence, and a deadline. The enforcement system has a quota and a
schedule; the person has a calendar and a fine.

**Bike-share rebalancing**
A rider wants a bike at a station that's empty. Behind the scenes, a truck
is moving bikes between stations based on a model. The rider sees a map; the
operator sees a forecast.

**Paratransit booking**
A rider with a disability books a ride 24 hours ahead. The system assigns a
vehicle, a time window, and a route. The rider waits in a window; the
dispatcher optimizes. Data hook: on-time performance, trip denials, wait times.

## Healthcare and social services

**Clinic intake**
A patient arrives, checks in, waits, is called, is seen, is discharged. The
front desk has a schedule; the patient has a number. Walk-ins and
appointments compete for the same slots.

**Food pantry distribution**
A person arrives during a window. The pantry has inventory, eligibility
rules, and a line. The person doesn't know what's available until they're
inside. Data hook: items distributed, wait times, no-shows.

**Prescription refill**
A refill requires a doctor's approval, a pharmacy's stock, and an insurance
check. The patient sees "pending." Three separate systems have to agree.

**Emergency room triage**
A person arrives. A nurse assigns a priority. The person waits. The waiting
room has a visible order that may not match the actual order. Data hook:
wait times by acuity, left-without-being-seen rates.

## Education

**Course registration**
A student needs specific courses to graduate. Sections fill. Prerequisites
block. The student has a cart and a timer; the registrar has a capacity model.

**Financial aid verification**
A student submits documents. The office requests more. Deadlines approach.
The student doesn't know what's missing until they check. Data hook:
verification rates, document rejection reasons, processing times.

**Advising appointment**
A student books 15 minutes. The advisor has a caseload and a degree audit
tool. The student has questions; the advisor has a checklist. The meeting
ends before everything is covered.

---

## Work and employment

**Shift scheduling**
A worker requests time off. A manager builds a schedule around coverage.
The worker sees the final schedule; the manager sees requests, conflicts, and
budget. Data hook: coverage gaps, overtime, swap frequency.

**Payroll / timesheet approval**
An employee submits hours. A supervisor approves. A payroll system processes.
An error in any step delays payment. The employee sees "pending"; the
supervisor sees a queue.

**Hiring / interview loop**
A candidate applies. A recruiter screens. A panel interviews. A hiring
manager decides. Each step has a different owner and a different timeline.
The candidate sees silence.

**Expense reimbursement**
An employee submits a receipt. It's reviewed, approved, and paid. Each
step has a different owner. The employee tracks a status; the system tracks
a workflow.

---

## Retail and commercial

**Return / exchange**
A customer returns an item. The system checks the purchase, the condition,
the policy, and the inventory. The customer sees a counter; the system sees
a transaction chain.

**Order fulfillment / pickup**
A customer orders. A warehouse picks. A courier delivers. The customer sees
a status; each handoff has a different owner and a different delay.

**Price adjustment / price match**
A customer finds a lower price. The store checks policy, competitor, and
receipt. The employee has discretion; the system has rules. The customer
waits for a decision.

---

## Community and neighborhood

**Block party / event permit**
A resident applies for a street closure. The city checks insurance,
neighbors, and traffic. The resident collects signatures; the city collects
fees. Timeline: 30 days, unknown status in between.

**Community garden plot assignment**
A waitlist exists. Plots open when someone leaves. The gardener doesn't
know when. The coordinator has a map and a list. Data hook: turnover, plot
sizes, wait times.

**Lost and found / item recovery**
Someone loses something. A system (transit, school, venue) logs it. The
person files a claim. The system matches descriptions. Success depends on
how both sides described the item.

**Recycling / waste collection complaint**
A missed pickup is reported. The complaint goes to a dispatcher, then a
driver, then back to the resident. The resident sees "submitted"; the
system sees a route.

---

## Events and coordination

**Conference check-in**
Attendees arrive in waves. Badges are printed or pre-printed. Sessions
have rooms and capacities. The organizer sees a dashboard; the attendee
sees a line.

**Volunteer shift coordination**
A coordinator assigns shifts. Volunteers confirm, swap, or no-show. The
coordinator has a spreadsheet; the volunteer has a text message. Coverage
gaps appear at the last minute.

**Meeting room booking**
A room is booked. Someone else needs it. The system has rules; people have
workarounds. The conflict is resolved by whoever asks first or whoever is
loudest.

## Digital systems

**Password reset / account recovery**
A user forgets a password. The system sends a link to an email. The email
may be old. The user has a time limit; the system has a token expiry. Each
failure restarts the flow.

**Content moderation queue**
A post is flagged. A reviewer reads it, applies a rule, and decides. The
reviewer has a queue and a quota; the poster has a notification and an
appeal path. Data hook: review times, appeal rates, category accuracy.

**Bug report / support ticket**
A user files a ticket. It's triaged, assigned, and resolved. The user
sees a status; the support team sees a priority and a backlog. Data hook:
resolution times, reopen rates, category volume.

**Subscription cancellation**
A user wants to cancel. The system requires steps: login, navigate,
confirm, possibly a retention offer. The system has friction; the user has
patience. Data hook: cancellation completion rates, time-to-cancel.

## How to pick

Ask your group three questions:

1. **Can we observe it?** Can we watch it happen, talk to someone who does
   it, or find a public record of it this week?
2. **Whose perspective is interesting?** The person moving through, the
   person processing, or the person waiting? Pick one.
3. **What would the player need to know?** If the answer is "nothing, they
   just click," it's not a system — it's a clicker. Find a different one.

The best systems for this sprint have a dependency the player can't see at
first but can learn. That's where the game lives.
