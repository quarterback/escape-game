# COALITION: confirmed game direction

**Confirmed by the owner:** October 29, 2026  
**Status:** Design direction for future development. The shipped Last Freight Out prototype does not yet implement this structure.

Read this document before changing gameplay, progression, narrative, cards, or the interface. Earlier build reports describe the prototype and its history; they do not override the direction recorded here. Proposed details below remain proposals unless the owner selects them.

## The game in one paragraph

You play a person arranging their escape through several connected levels. Money and powerful contacts give you options, but you still depend on people who control different parts of the journey. You must coordinate their help while enemies interfere. Each level presents an immediate situation that gets you farther along the road, while other arrangements continue elsewhere. Earlier choices determine the help, documents, commitments, obligations, and risks you carry forward. There is one playable border crossing near the end. Crossing does not finish the escape: you must reach the jet, depart, and leave the relevant airspace to win.

## The central challenge

Several people must deliver different parts of the escape, and you have to keep those arrangements working together while moving toward the border.

A sponsor can secure an audience without guaranteeing a host's answer. A relative can arrange an aircraft without getting you to it. An official can help with one procedure without controlling the next office. A driver can complete a journey only if the reception and timing work.

Money, influence, documents, physical movement, and permission have different uses. Having one does not automatically supply the others. Allies have conditions and limits; enemies retain the ability to interfere.

The story must help the player understand those differences and act on them.

## Levels and the complete run

- A run contains several distinct, playable levels leading toward escape.
- Each level has a scene, a clear immediate objective, and interactions that move the journey forward.
- Different approaches and changing circumstances should make levels replayable.
- The owner described roughly five to seven levels before or including the border encounter; the exact count remains open.
- There is only one playable border crossing, near the end.
- After crossing, a final escape stage functions like a bonus level in presentation, but remains necessary to win.
- Victory requires reaching the aircraft, departing, and leaving the relevant airspace.
- The destination's willingness to receive the person can be arranged during the run without adding another playable border encounter.

The level sequence is not yet selected. Do not turn an illustrative list of locations into a mandatory campaign.

## Preparation happens while you travel

Do not divide the game into a long preparation checklist followed by movement. Aircraft, reception, documents, and ground transport can be arranged while the player is already traveling. That overlap creates tension.

Each level connects two parts:

1. **The local problem:** what you must accomplish to move on.
2. **The continuing arrangements:** what allies and enemies are doing elsewhere, and what needs your decision.

For example, you might be trying to leave a house unnoticed when your relative calls: the aircraft is secured, but the operator needs a destination confirmed. You can spend attention on that now, delegate it, or keep moving and accept the uncertainty. Later, you discover whether the person you trusted delivered.

This is an illustration of the confirmed structure, not a prescribed opening level.

## The story is information you can use

Conversations and visible changes should explain:

- What someone controls.
- What they need before they will act.
- What they have actually confirmed.
- Why they might withdraw or fail.
- What their action makes possible next.

Use clear, concrete updates: driver confirmed; receiving contact not answering; aircraft waiting; passport available; departure restriction circulating.

A promise and a completed arrangement are different states. Players should have enough information to understand why an outcome followed, including when an ally's claim turns out to be unreliable.

Your actual location and your enemies' beliefs can differ. Concealment can buy time. The player need not know everything, but uncertainty should belong to the situation and story.

## Cards, relationships, and replay

The owner wants roguelike variation across levels and runs, with Balatro as a reference for progressing through a run and developing useful combinations.

Cards can represent specific usable help: an introduction, a vehicle, a document, information, access, or a promise to delay a report. Relationships can persist across levels while particular offers are used, expire, or change.

Earlier choices should change later options. An official's help, a second passport, and a driver's access might work together as a strong plan. An enemy forcing a route change could make part of that plan less useful.

The exact deck structure, draw rules, combinations, rewards, and progression remain open. A shop, energy system, poker-style scoring, or permanent upgrades are not established requirements.

## Uncertainty should create decisions

The owner wants dice rolls or another source of variation that makes each level dynamic. The exact mechanism remains open.

A working design principle from the confirmed explanation: uncertainty can change the problem and test cooperation, while preparation gives the player ways to respond.

A delayed driver might cost a departure window. A warning could let you leave earlier. A backup driver could absorb the setback. The player then chooses whether to wait, expose a backup contact, or accept another person's terms.

At the border, documents, exposure, contacts, and earlier commitments should matter visibly. A difficult outcome could force the player to spend help they hoped to save for the aircraft.

These examples do not establish probabilities, dice counts, or failure rules.

## What the current prototype misses

Last Freight Out currently provides one panoramic town, five stops on a fixed route, a shuffled action deck, and an immediate win on boarding a freight train.

It offers alternative ways to unlock local obstacles. It does not yet provide the confirmed journey through distinct levels, continuing arrangements across locations, or the border-to-aircraft ending.

The town could be considered as material for a future level. Its debt premise, protagonist, train ending, seven-hour clock, escalating bribe prices, heat rules, and precise card economy are prototype choices, not requirements for the new structure.

The visual redesign was paused while this direction was clarified. Styling should support the revised game rather than reinforce the prototype's structure.

## Fiction and source evidence

The supplied Wall Street Journal article inspired the coordination problem. It is not a required plot or a complete account of every handoff.

The supplied text reports decoys, remote charter arrangements, assistance from Argentine officials, an Italian passport on the flight manifest, and arrangements for U.S. admission. It does not establish who inside Brazil helped, whether officials deliberately ignored restrictions, or whether bribery occurred. An alternative passport's presence does not explain exactly how the departure restriction was handled.

Internal government allies, obstruction, invented permissions, and other mechanisms are available for the fictional game. Identify them as authored fiction rather than inferred historical facts.

The game may invent cities, institutions, characters, routes, and political circumstances. A destination elsewhere in the Nelosphere world, including a place outside the Nelosphere countries, is possible. Zaryanova was mentioned as a possibility, not selected as the destination.

## Next design work

Define one complete level: what the player sees, which offers or cards are available, what they can commit, how uncertainty changes the situation, and what carries forward. Connect it to a later scene that demonstrates a consequence.

Keep these questions open until selected: level count and sequence, character and destination, turn costs, resources, deck rules, dice, enemy behavior, failure and recovery, and progression between runs.

Do not add repeated document inspection, clerk quotas, or a help-versus-block campaign based solely on earlier exploratory reports. The current direction places the player in the escaping person's position.
